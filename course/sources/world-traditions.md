# World traditions: a short list, with a search for open materials

A research note for the course, written 2026-11-09. It picks well preserved musical traditions that suit an elementary classroom and, for each, looks for materials that are safe to reuse: public domain, CC0, CC BY, CC BY-SA, or clearly licensed for educational reuse. Where possible it prefers materials from the culture's own institutions, musicians and community organisations over colonial-era Western transcriptions, and it says where only the latter could be found. Licence statements are quoted as the source states them; "unstated" means the page carries no licence, "unverified" means the page could not be fetched on the date above and the licence is reported second hand.

Verdicts: **use freely** (public domain or CC0/CC BY/CC BY-SA, compatible with this AGPL project), **use with attribution** (CC BY family, credit required), **ask permission** (free to view but no reuse licence, or a non-commercial licence that does not fit this project), **reference only** (read it, learn from it, type your own transcription by ear if the melody is traditional, but copy nothing).

One rule runs through everything below: a traditional melody or rhythm is nobody's copyright, but a particular recording, transcription, arrangement or translation is somebody's. Typing a pattern or tune by ear into `world.rhythms.txt` or an `.abc` file is always possible; copying someone's notation image or audio is not, unless the licence says so. Ceremonial and sacred material is a separate question that no licence settles.

## 1. North American Indigenous

### Anishinaabe (Ojibwe) song

**For the class.** Anishinaabe people live around the Great Lakes, in Ontario and in Minnesota, Wisconsin and Michigan. Song is carried by the voice and the drum: the big drum at a powwow played by several singers together, and the small hand drum for one singer. Many songs use vocables, syllables that carry the melody without being words, and the melody often starts high and descends in steps. Some songs are for everyone, like round dances, welcome songs and children's songs; others belong to ceremonies, to a lodge, or to a particular family, and those are not for a classroom. When we sing an Anishinaabe song we say where it came from and who shared it.

**Sources.**

- Ojibwe.net, Songs: https://ojibwe.net/songs. Traditional songs, children's songs (including Ojibwe-language versions of "Wheels on the Bus" and "Jingle Bells"), seasonal songs, notation for some pieces and choral arrangements made with a National Endowment for the Arts grant. Team: Alphonse Pitawanakwat, Stacie Sheldon, Margaret Noodin, Megan St. Andrew. Licence: unstated as a licence; the page says "All of the songs posted on this site are intended to be shared." and on choral scores "those scores may be copied for your ensemble with all copyright information included"; footer "Copyright 2025 Ojibwe.net". Verdict: use with attribution and tell the song's story; ask before republishing their notation.
- Musical Geography, Indigenous educator resources: https://musicalgeography.org/indigenous-educator-resources/. A curated list (Ojibwe.net, the Densmore Repatriation Project, Louis Ballard's 1973 classroom book, Wisconsin First Nations Ojibwe music). Statement: "These songs are freely shared by their originating Native communities and their (thoughtful) use is encouraged." It warns that scores should not be the primary teaching method because the songs are an aural tradition. Verdict: use as a guide.
- The Ojibwe People's Dictionary (University of Minnesota): https://ojibwe.lib.umn.edu/content/copyright-usage-restrictions. Audio of words and sentences by named speakers, useful for pronouncing song words. Licence page: content is copyrighted and "No material on this website may be used in whole or in part for commercial purposes, without the express written permission of The Ojibwe People's Dictionary"; the search summary reports a CC BY-NC-SA licence for adaptations. Verdict: ask permission (non-commercial clause does not fit AGPL redistribution); fine to link and play in class.
- Frances Densmore, *Chippewa Music* (BAE Bulletin 45, 1910) and *Chippewa Music II* (Bulletin 53, 1913): https://archive.org/details/chippewamusic00dens. Staff transcriptions of some 340 songs recorded at White Earth, Red Lake, Leech Lake and Lac du Flambeau, with texts and notes. Rights: "Possible copyright status: NOT_IN_COPYRIGHT" (US government publication). Verdict: use freely in law, but respectfully: many of the songs are Midewiwin, dream and healing songs recorded under conditions the singers' descendants did not agree to. Use the volumes to understand form and scale, pick only songs Densmore herself labels social, game or lullaby, cross-check with a living source such as Ojibwe.net, and name the original singer (Densmore records names) rather than Densmore alone.
- Densmore Repatriation Project (Standing Rock, Lakota): re-recordings of Densmore's 1911 Lakota cylinders by Lakota singers with lesson plans; statement reported in the search that materials are "freely available for non-commercial, educational use" and remain the cultural property of the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe. Not Anishinaabe, but the best model of how a community reclaims a colonial collection. Verdict: ask permission before redistributing; use for teaching about the process.

**Protocol.** Anishinabek Nation education materials (anishinabek.ca) are treaty and governance resources rather than song collections and carry no CC licence; order through the Nation. Follow OCAP (ownership, control, access, possession) in spirit: Indigenous communities decide what of theirs is shared. Do not use ceremonial, Midewiwin, pipe or sweat lodge songs; prefer songs a living Anishinaabe teacher has published for sharing.

### Inuit katajjaq (throat singing) and drum dance

**For the class.** Katajjaq is a game between two women standing face to face, trading short voiced and breathed sounds in a fast cycle until one laughs or runs out of breath. The sounds imitate geese, wind, sled runners and the sea. The drum dance uses the qilaut, a large flat frame drum struck on the rim with a stick while the dancer sings a pisiq, a personal song. Katajjaq was suppressed by missionaries and revived by Inuit women; it belongs to them, and it is taught today by Inuit performers such as those who advise school programmes.

**Sources.**

- MusiCounts, *Kanata: Contemporary Indigenous Artists and their Music*, teacher guide part 2 (2025): https://musicounts.s3.amazonaws.com/documents/MusiCounts_Kanata_part_2_-_2025.pdf. Free PDF; author Sherryl Sewepagaham with Inuit throat singer Kathy Kettler among cultural advisors; includes a katajjaq and beatboxing comparison. Licence: unstated. Verdict: ask permission to copy; use the activities.
- Inhabit Education, *Songs to Make You Smile* (Arvaaq Press): https://inhabiteducationbooks.com/products/songs-to-make-you-smile. Inuktitut and English songbook from *Anaana's Tent*, sung by Rita Claire, with a digital download of the tracks. Commercial, Inuit-owned publisher in Iqaluit. Verdict: buy for the classroom; reference only for the project.
- Beatrice Deer, *Little Songs* (fifteen Inuktitut folk songs for children): https://nunatsiaq.com/stories/article/new-album-brings-inuktitut-folk-songs-to-children. Commercial recording by a Nunavik singer. Verdict: reference only.
- Government of Nunavut, Qilaut Inuktut children's songwriting contest: https://www.gov.nu.ca/en/newsroom/submit-childrens-songs-qilaut-2016-2016-08-26. Winning songs recorded professionally; licence unverified.
- Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami, Katiqsugat resource list tagged "songs": https://katiqsugat.itk.ca/items/browse?tags=songs. Could not be fetched (certificate error); unverified.

**Protocol.** No Inuit-led open licensed katajjaq teaching resource was found. Katajjaq should be demonstrated by or with Inuit performers (recordings or a visit), not taught by a non-Inuit teacher as a technique; the drum dance pisiq is a personal song and is not for reuse. Treat Wikipedia's text (CC BY-SA) as background only.

### Haudenosaunee social songs

**For the class.** The Haudenosaunee (Six Nations: Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca, Tuscarora) separate social dances from ceremonial ones. Social dances such as the Stomp (Standing Quiver), Women's Dance, Round Dance, Duck Dance, Alligator Dance and the newer competitive Smoke Dance are sung with a water drum and cow-horn rattle, often with a leader and group answering, and everyone in the community is welcome to dance. Ceremonial songs of the Longhouse are not performed outside it.

**Sources.**

- Native North American Travelling College (Akwesasne), Haudenosaunee Social Songs and Dances video gallery: https://nnatc.org/video-gallery/. Five teaching videos made by the travel troupe in 2025 (Stomp, Women's, Round, Duck, Alligator), sponsored by SSHRC and Carleton University; the College also sells a two-CD set and a book on the meaning of the songs. Licence: unstated. Verdict: ask permission; this is the Indigenous-led source to point teachers to.
- Earth Songs of the Seneca Nation: https://senecasongs.arca1650.info/songs.html. Explanations of social dance songs, their use in Smoke Dance competitions and powwows, and that they are learned orally. Licence: unstated. Verdict: reference only.
- Smithsonian Folkways FE 4072, Seneca social dance songs (Avery and Fidelia Jimerson), liner notes: https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/FW04072.pdf. Free liner notes PDF; recordings are commercial. Verdict: liner notes reference only.
- American Philosophical Society, William Fenton recordings at Six Nations of the Grand River (1945, 1948): https://indigenousguide.amphilsoc.org/entry/38327. Mostly social dance songs; APS says Open Access Materials may be used for non-commercial, scholarly, educational or fair use, with some items restricted or flagged culturally sensitive. Verdict: ask permission.

**Protocol.** Use social songs only, say which nation and community they come from, and prefer the Travelling College's own teaching. Nothing from the Midwinter ceremony or False Face society.

## 2. Caribbean

### Trinidad calypso and steelpan

**For the class.** Calypso grew from the kaiso of enslaved Africans in Trinidad, sung by a calypsonian who comments on news and politics with wit, over a rhythm in 2/4 or 4/4 with a lifted second beat. The steelpan was invented in the 1930s and 40s in Port of Spain by young people who tuned oil drums into melody instruments; a steelband has tenor pans for melody, double seconds and guitars for chords and basses, with an "engine room" of iron, scratcher and drum kit. It is the national instrument of Trinidad and Tobago and the only acoustic instrument family invented in the twentieth century.

**Sources.**

Lesson plans with streaming audio. Licence: unstated on the page; a Folkways teacher guide elsewhere (SFLN067) says "personal, educational, and non-commercial uses allowed; commercial rights reserved." Verdict: use in class; ask permission before copying into the project.
- My Learning (UK), "Playing steel pans at Leeds West Indian Carnival": https://www.mylearning.org/resources/playing-steel-pans-at-leeds-west-indian-carnival. Licence as reported in the search: Creative Commons BY-NC-SA. Verdict: ask permission (non-commercial).

### Jamaican mento and ring games

**For the class.** Mento is Jamaica's country dance music from before ska and reggae: banjo, guitar, hand drum, a rhumba box (a big thumb piano that plays the bass) and sometimes a bamboo saxophone, with a swinging 4/4 that stresses the last beat. Ring games are children's singing games played in a circle in the yard or schoolyard, like "Brown Girl in the Ring" and "Jane and Louisa". Jamaican folklorist Louise Bennett (Miss Lou) recorded these games and the Anancy stories that go with them so that children would keep them.

**Sources.**

- Walter Jekyll (comp.), *Jamaican Song and Story* (1907): https://gutenberg.org/ebooks/35410. Anancy stories, digging sings, ring tunes and dancing tunes, with melodies in staff notation and texts. Project Gutenberg: "This eBook is for the use of anyone anywhere at no cost and with almost no restrictions whatsoever." Public domain. Verdict: use freely, as an English clergyman's 1907 transcription of what Black Jamaicans sang to him; read the ring tunes beside Louise Bennett's recordings.
- Smithsonian Folkways, "Sound Island: Jamaican Classroom": https://folkways.si.edu/sound-island-jamaican-classroom/world/music/tools-for-teaching/smithsonian. Lesson pairing Jekyll's book with Louise Bennett's recordings of children's songs and games ("Rocky Road", "Jane and Louisa"). Licence: unstated; see Folkways note above. Verdict: use in class; reference only for the project.
- Wikipedia, "Jamaican folk music" and "Songs of Jamaica": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaican_folk_music. CC BY-SA text for background.

### Puerto Rican bomba and plena

**For the class.** Bomba is the oldest Afro-Puerto Rican music, from the sugar plantations: two or three barrel drums (the low buleador keeps the rhythm, the high primo answers the dancer), a cuá of sticks on wood, a maraca, and a singer with a chorus. The dancer leads and the primo drummer follows every move of the skirt and feet. Plena is a hundred years younger, from the working neighbourhoods of Ponce, a "sung newspaper" with hand-held panderos (frame drums) in three sizes, a güiro, and verses about the day's news. Both are community music, played at the batey or in the street, and are strongly alive in Puerto Rican communities in New York, Chicago and Toronto.

**Sources.**

- Smithsonian Folkways, "Bomba" lesson (grades 3 to 8, Sandra Sanchez): https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/lesson_plans/FLP10112_Bomba.pdf. Movement and drumming activities around Viento de Agua's "Siré y Siré". Licence: unstated in the search result; the related teacher guide reads "personal, educational, and non-commercial uses allowed; commercial rights reserved." Verdict: use in class; ask permission to redistribute.
- Smithsonian Folkways, "Plena: The People's Newspaper in the Puerto Rican Classroom": https://folkways.si.edu/plena-peoples-newspaper-puerto-rican-classroom/music/tools-for-teaching/smithsonian, with Smithsonian videos of Los Pleneros de la 21 demonstrating the panderos: https://si.edu/es/object/los-pleneros-de-la-21-demonstrate-drums-used-plena:yt_upxObkVB0UM. Verdict: use in class; reference only.
- Kennedy Center, "Tap Bomba in the House with Sarah and Samuel": https://www.kennedy-center.org/video/education/music-world/tap-bomba-in-the-house-with-sarah-and-samuel/. Home-made instruments. Licence: unstated. Verdict: reference only.

### Haitian song

**For the class.** Haitian Creole folk song includes work songs sung by konbit (cooperative work teams) with a lead singer and answering group, children's songs, carnival rara with bamboo trumpets (vaksin) and drums, and the sacred drumming of Vodou, which is religious and is not classroom material. Rhythms often set three against two.

**Sources.**

- Harold Courlander, *Haiti Singing* (1939), songs in Creole and English with melodies: catalogue record https://libcat.library.tamu.edu/Record/in00000723666. US copyright status not confirmed (1939 book; renewal unknown). Verdict: reference only until checked.

- Duke University, Radio Haiti archive, "Pawòl ak Mizik" programmes: https://repository.duke.edu/dc/radiohaiti/RL10059-RR-1665_01. CC licensed per the search, with a warning that some material is not covered. Verdict: check item by item; ask permission.

## 3. Afro-Cuban and Brazilian

### Cuban rumba and son clave

**For the class.** Rumba was born in the poor neighbourhoods of Havana and Matanzas among Afro-Cuban dockworkers, with three conga drums (or packing crates), claves and a palito stick pattern on the side of a drum, a singer and chorus, and dancers. Yambú is slow, guaguancó has the famous chase between the dancers, columbia is a fast solo for men. The clave is a five-stroke pattern across two bars that everything else locks into; son clave (3-2 or 2-3) is the one most Latin music uses. UNESCO inscribed rumba on its Representative List in 2016.

**Sources.**

- UNESCO ICH, "Rumba in Cuba, a festive combination of music and dances and all the practices associated": https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/rumba-in-cuba-a-festive-combination-of-music-and-dances-and-all-the-practices-associated-01185. Description, photographs "© National Council for Cultural Heritage, 2014" and video "© AHS, 2015". Licence of the text: links to UNESCO's Terms of Use, not fetched; unverified. Verdict: reference only, a good read-aloud.
- Eduardo Sánchez de Fuentes, *El folk-lor en la música cubana* (Havana: Imprenta El Siglo XX, 1923). No digital copy was found (archive.org, HathiTrust, BBM searches returned only citations). Copyright: the author died in 1944, so public domain in Canada (life+50 at the time, and the 2022 extension was not retroactive) and in the US (published 1923). Verdict: use freely if a scan turns up, but critically: later Cuban musicologists (Carpentier, Ortiz) showed that he understated the African contribution and invented an Indigenous one.
- Wikipedia, "Eduardo Sánchez de Fuentes": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eduardo_S%C3%A1nchez_de_Fuentes. CC BY-SA background.

The clave, cáscara and tumbao patterns are traditional; type them.

### Brazilian samba, maracatu and folk melodies

**For the class.** Samba is Brazil's carnival music, from the Afro-Brazilian communities of Bahia and Rio: a bateria of surdos (big bass drums answering each other), caixas, tamborins, agogô bells, cuíca and the shaker, with a 2/4 that leans on the second beat. Maracatu de baque virado from Recife is older and slower, a procession of a king and queen with alfaias (rope-tuned bass drums), gonguê bell and shakers. Brazilian folk melodies were collected in the 1920s and 30s by Brazilian musicians, above all Mário de Andrade, who argued that Brazil's art music should grow from them.

**Sources.**

- Mário de Andrade, *Ensaio sobre a música brasileira* (São Paulo: I. Chiarato, 1928), scan by the Biblioteca Brasiliana Guita e José Mindlin: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ensaio_sobre_a_Musica_Brasileira_(1928).pdf. Essay plus a collection of folk melodies in staff notation (the 1962 edition expanded it to about 120 documents). Licence: "Creative Commons Public Domain Mark 1.0"; "The author died in 1945, so this work is in the public domain in its country of origin"; public domain in the US as a 1928 publication and in Canada. Verdict: use freely.
- Luciano Gallet, *Estudos de folclore* (Rio de Janeiro: Carlos Wehrs, 1934), ed. Mário de Andrade: https://imslp.org/wiki/Estudos_de_folclore_(Gallet,_Luciano). Six dances with instructions (cateretê, caninha verde, jongo, samba, cabôclo, canôa) and nineteen folk tunes collected 1927 to 1928. IMSLP lists it as "Public Domain" in Canada and the EU; the search summary adds IMSLP's note that it is "probably still protected by copyright in the United States" (US term for a 1934 publication runs to 1 January 2030). Verdict: use freely in Canada, where this project is made; note the US date in the file header.

### Capoeira songs

**For the class.** Capoeira is an Afro-Brazilian game of movement, music and song played in a circle, the roda. The berimbau (a bow with a gourd) leads, with pandeiro, atabaque drum and agogô; the mestre sings a ladainha (a solo litany), then call and response corridos that everyone answers while two players move inside the circle. The songs tell of enslavement, of mestres and of the game itself. Capoeira is practised in Toronto and across Ontario, and a local group is the best teacher.

**Sources.**

- Wikipedia, "Capoeira music" and "Ladainha": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capoeira_music. CC BY-SA text with song types and sample texts. Verdict: use with attribution (text only).
- Academic collections of ladainhas and corridos, e.g. "A linguagem das ladainhas de capoeira" (USP, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0): https://revistas.usp.br/esse/pt_BR/article/view/49186, and a PUC-Rio dissertation with a "Coletânea de cantos da capoeira": https://www.maxwell.vrac.puc-rio.br/14992/14992_1.PDF (licence unstated). Verdict: ask permission for the collections; the traditional song texts themselves are free to type.

No open licensed capoeira songbook was found; commercial compilations (e.g. *Canta Capuêra*) are in copyright.

## 4. West African

### Ewe and Ga drumming (Ghana and Togo)

**For the class.** Among the Ewe of south-eastern Ghana and Togo, music is a drum ensemble: the gankogui double bell plays the timeline that everyone measures against, the axatse rattle doubles it, and the drums (kagan, kidi, sogo, the master drum atsimevu) speak in phrases, with singing and dance. Agbekor is an old war dance, now for festivals and funerals; Gahu is a social dance borrowed from Nigeria. The Ga of Accra created Kpanlogo in the 1960s as youth music, and its bell pattern and conga-style drums are a favourite school repertoire worldwide. The twelve-pulse bell pattern is the same shape as many Caribbean and Cuban patterns.

**Sources.**

- Wikimedia Commons, Category:Music of Ghana: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Music_of_Ghana. Audio files "Kpanlogo Rhythmus.wav", "Fumefume.ogg", "Gankogui standard pattern.mid", "Ghanaian gyil.mid", subcategories Astiagbekor and Bells in Ghana; "Sample video of Ghanaian traditional music.webm" is "Creative Commons Attribution 3.0 Unported" per the search result. Licence: per file; the others were not individually checked. Verdict: use with attribution, after reading each file page.
- C.K. Ladzekpo, UC Berkeley Music 148 African Music Ensemble: https://www.richardhodges.com/ladzekpo/music148syllabus.html. Transcriptions of Kpanlogo, Gahu and Gamamla bell pieces, song books for Kpanlogo, Takada, Gahu, Sovu and Tokoe, an essay on dance drumming, videos. Written by an Anlo-Ewe master drummer. ### Akan children's songs

**For the class.** Akan (Asante, Fante) children's play songs are sung in Twi in circle and clapping games, with call and response, often on a pentatonic scale, and they teach counting, manners and stories. Adults' music includes adowa and kete court drumming and the talking drums that speak Twi proverbs.

**Sources.**

- Ruth Finnegan, *Oral Literature in Africa* (Open Book Publishers, 2012), chapter "Children's Songs and Rhymes": https://openbookpublishers.com/books/10.11647/obp.0025/chapters/10.11647/obp.0025.11. ### Èwè-Dòmè storytelling songs (Ghana)

- Divine Kwasi Gbagbo and D. Rose Elder, "Storytelling Songs of the Èwè-Dòmè of Ghana," *African Music* 11(1), 2019, pp. 91-112: https://doi.org/10.21504/amj.v11i1.2294. ### Yoruba and Mande music

**For the class.** Yoruba music of Nigeria and Benin is built on the dùndún talking drums, hourglass drums whose pitch bends to imitate the three tones of the Yoruba language, together with bàtá drums and bells, and praise singing. Mande music of Mali, Guinea and Senegal centres on the jeli (griot) who keeps history in song with the kora (a 21-string harp), the balafon and the ngoni; the djembe and dunun drums accompany dances for harvests and weddings. Both traditions are among the most shared West African musics in the world.

### Hungarian folk song (Bartók and Kodály)

**For the class.** Béla Bartók and Zoltán Kodály carried a phonograph into Hungarian villages from 1905 and recorded thousands of peasant songs, then sorted them by rhythm and scale. The oldest layer is pentatonic with descending lines, sung in free "parlando" rhythm; newer songs have a bouncy "tempo giusto" dance rhythm for the csárdás. Kodály built a whole school music method on these children's songs, which is why Ontario classrooms sing so-mi songs with hand signs. The Hungarian Academy of Sciences now puts Bartók's own card system online.

### Jewish (Yiddish and Ladino)

**For the class.** Yiddish folk songs come from the Jewish towns of Eastern Europe: lullabies, love songs, work songs and the dance tunes that klezmer bands played at weddings with clarinet, fiddle and cimbalom, often in the freygish scale with its raised third. ## 6. Arabic and Middle Eastern

### Arabic maqam and children's songs

**For the class.** Arabic music is melody and rhythm without chords: a maqam is a scale with a mood and typical phrases, some with notes between the piano's keys (quarter tones), and an iqa' is a rhythm cycle spoken with the syllables dum (low) and tak (high) on the darbuka or riqq. The takht ensemble has oud, qanun, nay flute, violin and percussion, and singers answer each other in call and response. Children's songs across the Arab world use maqam Bayati and Rast, simple iqa'at like maqsum and malfuf, and clapping games.

### Turkish makam and türkü

**For the class.** Turkish classical music uses makams, scales with many small intervals (a whole tone is divided into nine commas), and usuls, rhythm cycles clapped on the knees as düm and tek. Türkü are the folk songs of Anatolia, sung with the bağlama (saz) long-necked lute, often in "aksak" (limping) metres like 9/8 grouped 2+2+2+3, which is also the rhythm of the karşılama dance. Turkish Radio and Television (TRT) built a repertoire of about 5,000 notated türkü for its broadcasts, which every Turkish music student learns.

### Persian radif and dastgah

**For the class.** Persian classical music is learned from the radif, a collection of about 250 short melodies (gusheh) grouped into twelve modal systems called dastgah and avaz, memorised from a master over years and then used as the basis for improvisation. Instruments include the tar and setar lutes, santur hammered dulcimer, kamancheh spike fiddle, ney and the tombak goblet drum. Rhythms are often free, following the metre of classical poetry; dance pieces are in 6/8.

## 7. South Asian

### Hindustani and Carnatic rhythm (tala and konnakol)

**For the class.** Indian classical music has two traditions, Hindustani in the north and Carnatic in the south. Both build melody from a raga and rhythm from a tala, a cycle of beats shown with claps and waves of the hand. In the south, drummers learn rhythm by speaking it: konnakol syllables like ta ka di mi (four) and ta ki ta (three) let a student say a whole mridangam solo before playing it. Hindustani tabla players do the same with bols like dha dhin dhin dha for the sixteen-beat teental. These spoken rhythms are perfect for a classroom with no instruments.

### Rabindrasangeet

**For the class.** Rabindranath Tagore (1861 to 1941), the Bengali poet who won the Nobel Prize in 1913, wrote more than two thousand songs, words and melodies together, mixing Hindustani ragas, Bengali folk tunes (Baul and kirtan) and even Scottish and Irish airs he heard in England. ## 9. South-East Asian

### Javanese and Balinese gamelan

**For the class.** A gamelan is an orchestra of bronze gongs and metallophones tuned together, so no instrument can be swapped with another set's. In Central Java the gong cycle is slow and layered: the big gong marks the end of each cycle, kenong and kempul divide it, the saron family plays the core melody (balungan), and faster instruments and singers decorate it. Music is written in kepatihan cipher notation (1 to 7). In Bali the kebyar style is fast and bright, with pairs of instruments tuned slightly apart to shimmer and interlocking parts (kotekan) split between two players. Gamelan accompanies shadow puppets, dance and temple ceremonies; there are community gamelans in Toronto and many Ontario universities.

### Philippine folk songs and Tinikling

**For the class.** Filipino folk songs mix Spanish and Indigenous roots: "Bahay Kubo" names the vegetables around a nipa hut, "Leron Leron Sinta" is a playful courting song, and the rondalla string band of bandurria, laud, guitar and bass plays them. Tinikling is the bamboo dance: two people clap long poles together on the ground in a 3/4 pattern (tap, tap, close) while dancers step in and out like the tikling bird in the rice fields, and the pace speeds up until everyone laughs. In the south the kulintang gong row of the Maguindanao and Maranao peoples is a different, older tradition.

**Sources.**

### Thai, Khmer and Lao: the Sadhukarn

**For the class.** Thailand, Cambodia and Laos share a court music of tuned percussion: the Thai piphat and Khmer pinpeat ensembles have xylophones (ranat, roneat), gong circles, oboe and drums, playing a main melody that every instrument varies at once. "Sadhukarn" (Sathukan) is the sacred opening piece played to greet the Buddha and the teachers before a performance in all three countries, and its versions show how one melody travels across borders. Vietnamese dân ca (folk song) and the đàn bầu one-string zither are a separate tradition.

**Sources.**

- Sadhukarn notation dataset (Zenodo 7248870): described in *Data* 2022 (https://opendata.uni-halle.de/bitstream/1981185920/112414/1/data-07-00150-v3.pdf). 25 versions of the main melody from Thailand, Cambodia and Laos in Western staff and Thai notation. Licence: dataset "CC0", paper CC BY 4.0. Verdict: use freely.

- CNRS CREM holds Vietnamese traditional recordings but marks them restricted. Reference only.

## 10. Polynesian and Pacific

### Hawaiian mele and hula

**For the class.** In Hawai'i a mele is a poem that is chanted (oli) or danced (hula). Hula kahiko, the old style, is danced to chant with the ipu heke gourd drum and pahu drum; hula 'auana, the modern style, has guitar, 'ukulele and steel guitar. The dancer's hands tell the story of the words: rain, a flower, a mountain, a chief. Mele were composed for people and places, and many belong to families or to hālau (hula schools), whose kumu hula decides who learns them. The ipu pattern for the basic kaholo step is already in the rhythm file.

**Sources.**

- Huapala: Hawaiian Music and Hula Archives (Kaiulani Kanoa-Martin): https://www.kalena.com/huapala. Lyrics with composer credits, MP3 and MIDI melodies, pages on hula instruments, steps and chants, plus Samoan and Tahitian songs. Notice: "Copyright © 1997 - 2010 Kaiulani Kanoa-Martin. All Rights Reserved." Individual songs by named composers are often still in copyright. Verdict: reference only.
- Nathaniel B. Emerson, *Unwritten Literature of Hawaii: The Sacred Songs of the Hula* (BAE Bulletin 38, 1909): https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/20299. Chant texts, translations and descriptions of hula types and instruments. Public domain (US government publication). Verdict: use freely in law, but it is a colonial-era book written during the overthrow and annexation years; many chants in it are sacred, so use it for instruments and step descriptions and not as a songbook.
- Hawaiian Music Hall of Fame: not reached this round; unverified.
- Ulukau, the Hawaiian Electronic Library: https://ulukau.org/. Hawaiian-language texts and dictionaries; terms not retrieved. Verdict: unverified.

**Protocol.** Choose hula 'auana songs with named living or recent composers only with permission, or older mele whose composer died before 1972 (public domain in Canada); never teach a chant from Emerson as a performance piece. Where possible, learn from a kumu hula.

### Māori waiata and haka

**For the class.** Waiata are Māori songs: waiata tangi laments, waiata aroha love songs, oriori lullabies that teach a child its whakapapa (genealogy), and the modern action songs (waiata-ā-ringa) sung with hand movements at school and on the marae. Haka are posture dances with chanted words, stamping and slapping, performed by women and men; the most famous, "Ka Mate", was composed by Te Rauparaha of Ngāti Toa around 1820, and New Zealand law now says who must be named when it is performed commercially. Kapa haka groups compete nationally, and every New Zealand school sings waiata.

**Sources.**

Verdict: text ask permission (NC); audio reference only.

Ask a local Māori community member (there are Māori groups in Toronto) where possible.

### Samoan and Tongan song and dance

**For the class.** Samoan music is choral: whole villages sing in harmony at church and at the fiafia night, and the sasa is a seated and standing group dance of fast, sharp arm movements in unison to a wooden slit drum (pate) or a rolled mat beaten with sticks. The siva is a graceful dance, the taualuga the finale danced by a chief's daughter. Tongan lakalaka is a standing group dance with poetry sung in parts, listed by UNESCO; the ma'ulu'ulu is seated. Pacific communities in Aotearoa publish children's songs in their languages for early learning.

## Protocol summary

- Ceremonial, sacred, lodge, Longhouse, Vodou, temple and funeral repertoire stays out, whatever its legal status.
- Songs owned by families, clans, hālau or iwi need the owner's permission; a licence on a website does not replace it.
- Indigenous data sovereignty (OCAP in Canada; the Ngāti Toa guardianship model in Aotearoa) means communities decide what is shared and how it is named. Credit the singer and community, not only the collector.
- Colonial-era public domain books (Densmore, Jekyll, Emerson, the 1890s Japanese and 1920s Philippine school series) are legally free; use them for form, instruments and context, choose social and children's items only, and put a living voice from the community beside them.
- Non-commercial Creative Commons licences (BY-NC, BY-NC-SA, BY-NC-ND) do not fit an AGPL project that others may reuse commercially; they are marked "ask permission" here even when free for a classroom.
