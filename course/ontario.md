# Ontario curriculum alignment

How the analyzers' levels relate to the progressions in *The Ontario Curriculum, Grades 1–8: The Arts* (2009, revised), Music and Dance strands, and *The Kindergarten Program* (2016). The alignment is in the analyzers as `ontario()` tables: each set entry now carries `ontario: { grade, band, concepts, all }`, the grade in which the curriculum first names a concept the pattern or step uses, with the concepts that put it there.

The short version: the music strand's duration concepts line up well with the rhythm lattice, and the dance strand's elements line up with what the step language can write, but the dance curriculum does not progress by step difficulty at all, so the two kinds of grading answer different questions and both are worth showing.

## What the curriculum says, grade by grade

Wording in quotation marks is the curriculum's own, from the "Fundamental concepts" boxes.

| Grade | Music: duration | Music: texture and form (layered parts) | Dance: the elements emphasised |
|---|---|---|---|
| K | "explore different elements (e.g., beat, sound quality, speed, volume) of music"; clap the beat; symbols for beats and rests | | "elements of … dance (e.g., rhythm, space, shape)"; creative movement; spatial awareness |
| 1 | "fast and slow tempi; rhythm versus beat; two and four beats per bar (2/4 and 4/4 metres); quarter note ('ta'), eighth note(s) ('ti-ti'), quarter rest; simple rhythmic ostinato" | "single melodic line in unison (monophony)"; "phrase, call and response" | **body and space**: body parts and zones, shapes, locomotor (galloping, skipping, rolling) and non-locomotor (hopping on one foot, jumping on two feet, kicking, bending knees, spinning); levels; directions forwards, backwards, sideways; tempo fast/slow, even/uneven rhythm; quality (percussive, sustained); mirroring a partner |
| 2 | "half note ('ta-ah'), half rest, whole note ('ta-ah-ah-ah'), whole rest" | "simple accompaniment (homophony), bordun patterns"; "simple melodic ostinato"; AB form | **body and space**: the right side against the left; pathways (straight, curvy, zigzag); diagonal directions; size of movement; tempo sudden, quick, sustained; force; quality (bouncing, shaking, smooth); shadowing a partner |
| 3 | "three beats per bar (3/4 metre), dotted half note, sixteenth-note patterns, sixteenth rest; presto, largo" | "simple two-part rounds, partner songs, canons"; ABA form | **time and energy**: force (lightness/strength), effort (pressing, gliding), quality (smoothly, cautiously, erratically, percussively); pattern forms AB, ABA, ABBA |
| 4 | "syncopation using an eighth note followed by a quarter note and an eighth note ('ti-ta-ti' or 'syn-co-pa'); … fermata" | "canon, simple two-part piece (simple polyphony)" | **time and energy**: symmetry/asymmetry, cross-lateral movement, gesture; pathways in air and on floor; rhythm steady/irregular, pause, stillness, duration; effort qualities (punch, thrust, float, collapse); meet/part, follow/lead, groupings |
| 5 | "dotted quarter note followed by an eighth note ('tam-ti'); dotted eighth note and sixteenth note ('tim-ka'); … 'tika-ti, ti-tika'; 6/8 metre ('one-and-a-two-and-a')" | "part singing (homophonic or polyphonic)"; rondo | **relationship**: group formations, pattern, proximity; call and response as a choreographic form; the swing and the salsa named |
| 6 | "9/8 metre … and other compound metres (e.g., 6/4); 5/4 metre; pick-up note(s) (anacrusis); triplets; … allegro, adagio" | "layering"; body percussion in a four-part round | all five elements: acceleration/deceleration; free flow; dancers and props; mirroring, flocking |
| 7 | "tempo markings …, rhythms in the repertoire they play and/or sing" | "major and minor triads"; 12-bar blues | choreographic forms: isolation of body parts (the shoulder alone), weight transfer (lunge, leap, roll); percussion, fluidity (wring, dab, flow, bind); stamping and body slapping to replicate rhythms; canon; jigs named |
| 8 | "tempo markings and rhythms encountered in the repertoire" | "monophonic, homophonic, and polyphonic music" | choosing the form; the waltz, swing, Celtic dance named |

Where a rhythm concept first appears: beat versus rhythm, 2/4 and 4/4, quarter and eighth notes, a rhythmic ostinato in grade 1; half and whole notes in grade 2; 3/4, sixteenth patterns in grade 3; syncopation in grade 4; dotted figures, mixed sixteenth cells and 6/8 in grade 5; 9/8, 6/4, 5/4, triplets and the anacrusis in grade 6; nothing new in grades 7 and 8. Layered parts progress from an ostinato (1) to a bordun under a line (2), rounds and partner songs (3), simple polyphony (4), part singing (5), layering and a four-part round (6).

Dance emphasises body and space in grades 1 and 2, time and energy in 3 and 4, relationship in 5, all five elements in 6, and elements with choreographic forms in 7 and 8. The curriculum names dance forms only as examples, never as a syllabus: folk, ceremonial, social and theatrical dances and hip hop in grade 2; the farandole and the hornpipe in grade 3; court and country dances and carnival dances in grade 4; the swing and the salsa in grade 5; Acadian, Cajun, French, Scottish and Métis jigs in grade 7; the waltz, Celtic dance, swing and disco in grade 8. No Ontario document assigns particular folk dances to grades.

## How the rhythm analyzer connects

The rhythm lattice's dimensions are subdivision, syncopation, parts, metre, length and tempo. Four of the six are the curriculum's own duration progression, nearly rung for rung:

| Rhythm lattice | Curriculum concept and grade |
|---|---|
| division: beats and halves | quarter and eighth notes, grade 1 |
| division: quarters (sixteenths) | sixteenth-note patterns, grade 3 |
| division: triplets | triplets, grade 6 (6/8 is grade 5) |
| syncopation: off-beat eighths left hanging | syn-co-pa, grade 4 |
| syncopation at sixteenth level | tim-ka, tika-ti, grade 5 |
| metre: 3/4 | grade 3 |
| metre: 6/8, 12/8 | grade 5 |
| metre: 5/4, 7/8, 9/8, 11/8 | grade 6 (5/4 is the only irregular metre named) |
| parts: one drum | rhythmic ostinato, grade 1 |
| parts: two, three, four or more | bordun (2), rounds and partner songs (3), simple polyphony (4) |
| polyrhythm (3 against 2) | not named; placed with triplets at grade 6 |

Two things the curriculum has that an onset grid cannot show: half and whole notes (grade 2) and the fermata (grade 4), because the grid writes when a sound starts, not how long it lasts. The Syllables view's fruit words recover long notes from a hit followed by silent beats (mel-on, wa-ter-mel-on), and that is where grade 2 lives in the display rather than in the analyzer.

Across the 197 patterns the two gradings agree in direction but not in detail: the lattice level and the Ontario grade move together (level 5 patterns are mostly grades 4 and 5, level 6 patterns grades 5 and 6), but the lattice also weighs tempo, length and how many parts a class must hold at once, which the curriculum does not. The practical reading: use the Ontario grade to say *which class may meet this*, and the lattice level to order the patterns *within* a class.

## How the tune analyzer connects

Tunes get the same duration reading from their ABC: half and whole notes put a tune at grade 2, a dotted half, sixteenths or 3/4 at grade 3, syncopation at 4, dotted pairs (tam-ti, tim-ka) and 6/8 at 5, triplets, 9/8 and 5/4 at 6, and notes shorter than a sixteenth at 7 (named only as "rhythms encountered in the repertoire"). Pitch, interval and key are not graded: the curriculum's pitch progression (high and low, contour, so-mi-la, pitch names, key signatures) is a separate reading that the tune lattice's pitch dimension does not map onto rung for rung, so the tune's grade says what its rhythm asks for and the lattice level the rest.

One placement to know about: the curriculum first names the pick-up note (anacrusis) in grade 6, so every tune with a pick-up sits at grade 6 whatever else it does. In the first set that is ten of the thirty-six tunes, among them Daisy Daisy and Highland Mary. That is where the Ministry put the concept, not a judgement about the tune; a class that has met pick-ups earlier can choose a higher grade on the board.

## How the dance analyzer connects

The dance curriculum's progression is in the elements of dance (body, space, time, energy, relationship) and in choreographic form, not in footwork. Nearly every step the language can write, including hops, jumps, kicks, skipping's uneven rhythm, and directions forward, back and sideways, is a grade 1 exploration. What raises the grade is the element a sequence brings in:

| Step language | Element and grade |
|---|---|
| `level:` line | levels, grade 1 |
| `Lf-H` two moves in a beat (skipping) | uneven rhythm, grade 1 |
| `turn:` line, `@` indirect | pathways and turning, grade 2 |
| `!` sudden, `~` sustained | tempo sudden/sustained, grade 2 |
| a second bar mirroring the first | the right side against the left, grade 2 |
| `_` strong, `^` light | force: lightness and strength, grade 3 |
| bars that repeat in a pattern | AB, ABA forms, grade 3 |
| `x` and `z` steps across | cross-lateral movement, grade 4 |
| `.` holds | pause and stillness, grade 4 |
| `=` bound flow | effort qualities, grade 4 |
| 6/8 | grade 5; irregular metres grade 6 (from the music strand) |
| `hips:` and `shoulders:` lines | isolation of body parts, grade 7 |

So the step lattice (feet, pace, arms, metre, length, tempo) and the Ontario grade are nearly independent: a grapevine is lattice level 6 (steps across) and grade 4 (cross-lateral); a merengue is level 3 and grade 7 (hip isolation); the march is level 1 and grade 1. Both are true. The lattice says how hard the feet are; the curriculum says which element the sequence teaches.

For the book dances the prose grader reads formation, interaction and figures, which are the curriculum's grade 4 and 5 relationship concepts (meet and part, follow and lead, group formations, changing partners), so almost every Burchenal dance lands at grade 5, and the three solo or ring dances at grade 2 (folk dances as a form). That matches how the curriculum would place them: longways and set dances are grade 5 relationship work regardless of their steps.

## What to do with it

- The sets carry both gradings, and the board and the pickers filter by the Ontario grade: **For** on the practice board, and the grade select beside the lab's and the main page's Example pickers, keep the items at or under a grade, grouped by lattice level within it. The choice is one setting shared by all three (the browser's `gcs-course-grade`).
- The Ontario grade is a floor, not a ceiling: a concept first named in grade 3 is taught from grade 3 on.
- CODE (the Council of Ontario Drama and Dance Educators, code.on.ca) publishes an elementary dance scope and sequence and grade units; its terms allow personal, non-commercial use only and forbid reproduction, so its material is a teacher's reference here, not a source for the project. The same goes for OMEA's members-only resources. The Ministry's curriculum text is what the tables quote.
- Named dance forms give a second, informal alignment for the typed world steps: swing and salsa to grade 5, jigs to grade 7, the waltz and Celtic steps to grade 8, folk dances generally to grade 2 and 3. These are examples in the curriculum, not requirements, and are left as notes rather than coded.
